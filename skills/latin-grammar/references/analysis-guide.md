# Grammar analysis conventions

Status: original teaching draft, not an externally reviewed grammar reference. Examples below are constructed; no edition or ancient source is claimed.

## Explain the relationship, not just the ending

Example: `Puella librum legit.` — “The girl reads/is reading a book.”

- `puella`: nominative singular here, functioning as subject.
- `librum`: accusative singular of `liber, libri` (book), functioning as object.
- `legit`: third-person singular present active indicative of `legere`, on the intended present-tense reading.

Without vowel-length marks and context, written `legit` can also represent the perfect `lēgit`. If tense ambiguity is relevant to the question, explain that “read/has read” may be possible; do not pretend the unmarked spelling uniquely fixes the tense.

## A targeted correction

Learner: `Puella librum legunt.` Intended meaning: “The girl reads a book.”

Minimal correction: `Puella librum legit.` In the intended present tense, singular `puella` takes singular `legit`; `legunt` is plural. Do not replace the sentence with a different topic.

Practice: “Several girls read a book.” One answer: `Puellae librum legunt.` Other word orders can be correct. Present the answer after an attempt or a request, not automatically with the question.

## A common ambiguity

`puellae` in isolation can be genitive singular, dative singular or nominative plural (also vocative plural). Context selects a reading. Do not translate every occurrence as “girls.”

`agricola, agricolae` is a first-declension masculine noun. Do not teach “all nouns ending in -a are feminine.” Ending, declension and gender are different properties.

## Scope and explanation language

Default to a basic classical grammatical explanation when no register is supplied, and state that choice only when it matters. Ecclesiastical pronunciation conventions do not by themselves change grammatical case analysis. Do not assess spoken pronunciation from plain text.
